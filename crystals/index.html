Phaöra  ·  Minas Gerais  ·  Selected work

Before it
was stone

it was water, and a room
with nothing in it.

Lava cools from the outside in. Gas trapped inside has nowhere to go, and what it leaves behind is a cavity — a void in solid rock, closed, empty, and in no hurry at all.

Then water finds it. Water carrying dissolved silica, moving so slowly that moving is a generous word for it, working through basalt across a span of time not worth trying to picture.

a room, sealed

The silica comes out of solution at the wall and starts building inward. Crystal by crystal, the room fills toward its own centre.

It has no plan and no hurry. It only has a wall to start from, and a middle to work toward.

and it begins,
at the wall, to build inward

The purple

Iron came in with the water, and the ground around it was radioactive enough to do the rest. That is all amethyst is — quartz with iron in it, that sat in the dark long enough for the dark to colour it.

The deepest purple sits at the tip, where the crystal grew last and slowest. Split one open and you can read its own calendar off the wall: pale where it started, saturated where it stopped.

colour is damage

And then it stops. Not because it was finished.

it ran out of room

The turn

A mason spends his working life arguing with rock about where it wants to break. The bed. The grain. The face that will take a chisel, and the face that will shatter if you ask it to.

After enough years you can look at a stone on a pallet and know, before you touch it, which way it opens.

That skill has no idea what scale it is working at. Point it at a two-ton step and it sets a step. Point it at a geode and it does exactly the same thing — reads the grain, finds the face, takes only what the stone is offering, and stops.

same eye,
different magnification

Most stone stops light.

That is the whole point of it. A wall is a thing light does not get through, which is why a mason thinks in shadow, in reveal, in what a chamfer does with a low sun in October.

These do the opposite. Light goes into them and comes back out somewhere else, and the carving has to be planned around it. Cut a wing too thin and it glows. Cut it too thick and it goes dead.

the one problem
a wall never gives you

7Quartz
against
4Fluorite

Fluorite cleaves perfectly in four directions at once, so there is no forgiving pass — one stroke with too much weight behind it takes a corner off, and there is no putting it back. It is why so little of it is ever carved into anything.

This one was not carved. It was lit, set on a stand, and left exactly as it came out of the ground.

already finished

Someone looked at it and decided that. It is the harder decision.

and then, once,

not a bird at all.

A vessel in pale blue-green stone, gold-brown veining running through it, and nothing else asked of it. No wings, no geode, no argument. Just the shape a bowl wants to be when the stone is allowed a say in it.
